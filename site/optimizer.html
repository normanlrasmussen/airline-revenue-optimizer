SIMULATION MODEL

The forecast is a best guess. The realized flight is noisier.

AeroYield deliberately separates the information used to build a policy from the random events that actually occur. The goal is a useful robustness experiment—not a claim that the airline knows the future demand process.

01 · FORECAST

What the policy sees

Baseline Saver/Main/Flex demand, generic booking curves, fares, physical capacity, and the selected booking limit. EMSR-b and DP are built once from this forecast.

02 · REALIZED WORLD

What changes each run

A shared market-demand shock, class-specific forecast error, smooth day-to-day booking bursts, timing shifts, random 1–4 seat parties, and customer attrition.

03 · DEPARTURE

What settles at the end

Cancellations release capacity before departure, no-shows leave unused seats only at departure, and excess show-ups after overbooking create refunds and denied-boarding costs.

METHODS

Math is available when you want it.

The controls deliberately separate what the policy forecasts from what actually occurs in the simulation.

Realized demand: correlated forecast error instead of independent noise

Each replication draws a market-wide demand shock and class-specific demand error. It also shifts booking timing and applies persistent day-level booking noise, so a strong or weak booking period tends to persist rather than producing unrelated spikes every day. These realized probabilities generate the requests; EMSR-b and DP never receive them.

Interpretation: the sliders define scenario uncertainty, not statistically calibrated airline forecast errors.

Customer attrition: cancellations and no-shows

Earlier bookings have more time to cancel, so their event-level cancellation probability is higher than bookings made close to departure. A cancellation releases its seats when it occurs and subtracts the selected refund fraction from net revenue. A no-show remains an active booking until departure, retains its ticket revenue in the current model, and can leave capacity unused if overbooking was insufficient.

EMSR-b: protection levels under an imperfect forecast

Fare classes are sorted from highest to lowest fare. Higher-fare forecast demand is aggregated and converted into nested protection levels. The realized simulation then perturbs demand level and booking timing and introduces party sizes and attrition, so EMSR-b does not receive the stochastic process that actually generates the requests.

Guarantee: EMSR-b remains a heuristic.

Dynamic programming: exact forecast model, approximate real-world policy

The DP state remains booking period and remaining booking capacity. Its Bellman recursion is solved exactly for the baseline one-seat forecast model. In the realized simulation, requests can contain multiple seats, arrival rates and timing differ from forecast, and cancellations/no-shows alter final capacity usage. Multi-seat requests are evaluated against the sum of the relevant forecast seat bid prices.

Guarantee: the DP is exact only for its internal forecast model. Its simulation performance is a robustness experiment, not an optimality claim for the richer realized world.

Oracle: perfect future information upper bound

The oracle knows the realized requests, which bookings cancel, and which customers no-show. It relaxes transient booking-limit constraints and selects the best show-up parties against physical capacity, so it is intentionally unattainable and used only as a perfect-information upper bound.
