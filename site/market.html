THE BUSINESS PROBLEM

A seat can only be sold once.

An airline has a fixed number of seats and does not know exactly who will book before departure. Selling every early request may fill the plane but leave no room for later customers willing to pay more. Protecting too many seats may leave the aircraft with empty inventory that becomes worthless at departure.

The decision: when a booking request arrives, should the airline accept it now or keep the seat available for possible future demand?
WHY IT IS HARD

The airline must decide before it knows the future.

  • Capacity is fixed: each departure has a limited number of seats.
  • Seats expire: an empty seat has no sale value after departure.
  • Demand is uncertain: future booking volume, timing, and fares are unknown.
  • Customers are different: some travelers book early at lower fares while others arrive later and may pay more.
  • Every sale changes the future: a seat sold now cannot be used for a later request.
WHAT AEROYIELD STUDIES

One flight, one booking horizon, one revenue objective.

AeroYield starts with a single economy cabin. Market data provides route context. A booking model generates uncertain customer requests. Revenue-management policies then decide which requests to accept.

Objective: maximize expected ticket revenue from a fixed seat inventory while accounting for demand that has not arrived yet.
WHAT MATTERS

Three inputs drive the decision.

DEMAND

Who may still book?

How many customers may arrive, when they arrive, and the fares they are willing to pay.

CAPACITY

How many seats remain?

Aircraft capacity, seats already sold, and time remaining before departure.

MARKET

What route are we selling?

Passenger volume, observed fares, competition, distance, seasonality, and recent market trends.

HOW SUCCESS IS MEASURED

A full plane is not automatically the best outcome.

REVENUE

Ticket revenue

How much revenue the flight earns under each policy.

LOAD

Seats filled

Load factor and empty seats show whether capacity was used or spoiled.

SPILL

Demand rejected

Rejected requests show the trade-off created by protecting capacity for later demand.
